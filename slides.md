<!--
Welcome. This lecture asks a simple question with a complicated answer: what is the garden actually doing in Nathaniel Hawthorne's “Rappaccini's Daughter”? We tend to treat setting as scenery — the wallpaper behind the real business of plot and character. Ignasi Ribó's textbook Prose Fiction argues otherwise. Setting, Ribó insists, is not a backdrop but a meaningful arrangement of the story's own spatial existents, as fully constitutive of narrative meaning as plot or character. Over the next several minutes, we will use Ribó's framework — principally his third chapter, on setting, supported by key ideas from his first two chapters, on narrative and plot — to show that Rappaccini's garden is not the stage on which this tragedy happens. It is the mechanism that produces the tragedy.
-->

<!--
Before we analyze anything, let's meet our two texts properly, in the spirit of good MLA practice: know what you're citing and why. Our primary text is Nathaniel Hawthorne's 1844 story 'Rappaccini's Daughter,' later collected in his 1846 volume Mosses from an Old Manse. It's set in Padua, home to one of the oldest university botanical gardens in the world, and it opens with a small, strange joke: Hawthorne presents the tale as a translation from a fictional French writer, 'Monsieur de l'Aubépine' — aubépine being French for 'hawthorn.' Keep that authorial disguise in mind; we'll return to it when we discuss verisimilitude. Our secondary text is Ignasi Ribó's 2019 textbook, Prose Fiction: An Introduction to the Semiotics of Narrative, published open access by Open Book Publishers under a Creative Commons license. Ribó organizes narrative theory around six elements — plot, setting, characterization, narration, language, and theme — and it's his third chapter, on setting, that will anchor almost everything we do today.
-->

<Cards cols="1">
<Card n="1">Ribó distinguishes the real author and reader from their textual projections — the implied author and implied reader (9).</Card>
<Card n="2">Inside the text, a narrator addresses a narratee; what passes between them is “discourse.”</Card>
<Card n="3">The content of that discourse is the “story” — built from three kinds of existents: events (plot), environments (setting), and characters (characterisation) (10).</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
Ribó's first chapter gives us the scaffolding the whole book runs on: the distinction between story and discourse. On one side stand the real people — Hawthorne the historical author, you the actual viewer. On the other side, inside the text itself, stand their textual projections: an implied author, a narrator, a narratee, and an implied reader. What that narrator communicates to that narratee is discourse; what gets communicated — the events, environments, and characters themselves — is story. Hawthorne, notably, plays with this very architecture: by attributing the tale to the invented Monsieur de l'Aubépine, he inserts an extra, fictional layer between himself and us, dramatizing exactly the implied-author distinction Ribó describes. And notice the three existents Ribó names as the content of story — events, environments, characters. Setting, in this model, isn't an accessory to plot and character. It's one of three co-equal building blocks of the storyworld itself. That claim is the whole reason we're here.
-->

<Cards cols="1">
<Card n="1">Ribó reads plot through Freytag's five stages — exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution (27).</Card>
<Card n="2">By Ribó's typology of “masterplots,” this is closest to tragedy: a protagonist “affected by some sort of mistake or flaw… ends with his failure” (25).</Card>

<!--
If setting is the spatial existent, plot is the temporal one — and Ribó insists we can't fully understand space in a story until we've located it in time. So, briefly: Hawthorne's plot maps cleanly onto Freytag's pyramid as Ribó presents it. Exposition introduces Giovanni's lodging above the garden. Rising action tracks his fascination with Beatrice, thickened by Baglioni's warnings. The climax is Giovanni's cruel accusation — the moment he treats Beatrice as a specimen rather than a person, the same sin he condemns in her father. Falling action is the procurement of the antidote; resolution is Beatrice's death. In Ribó's typology of masterplots, this is tragedy: a protagonist undone not by an external monster but by, quote, 'some sort of mistake or flaw' in his own character. Giovanni's flaw is suspicion — a scientific, clinical way of looking that turns out to be exactly Rappaccini's flaw as well. Once we know that shape, we can ask a sharper question: how does the garden itself drive that plot forward?
-->

<Card n="2">An environment is “everything that surrounds the characters” — landscapes, buildings, rooms, objects, plants (35).</Card>

<!--
So let's define our key term. Ribó writes, quote, 'if plot is the meaningful arrangement of the events of the story, setting can be conceived as the meaningful arrangement of the story's environments' — end quote. An environment, in his terms, is everything that surrounds the characters: landscapes, buildings, rooms, furniture, plants, weather. Hawthorne gives us an unusually contained example. Almost the entire story takes place in relation to a single environment: a walled garden in Padua, viewed first from above, through the window of the gloomy old palazzo where Giovanni takes lodging. That containment is not incidental. Ribó notes that some narratives unfold across vast, shifting environments — think of a novel that spans continents — while others deliberately confine themselves to one bounded space, the way Kafka traps Gregor Samsa in a single room. Hawthorne chooses confinement. The garden is Giovanni's whole world, and ours, for the length of the story. That confinement is the first clue that setting here is doing more work than mere backdrop.
-->

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Space and time are fused, not separate">Ribó reminds us that “space and time are intimately connected in what physicists call space-time continuum and literary theorists refer to as the chronotope” (34), citing Mikhail Bakhtin.</Card>
<Card n="2" head="Two chronotopes collide in this story">The university runs on linear, accumulating, rational time — lectures, experiments, progress. The garden runs on cyclical, ritual time: Rappaccini's daily rounds, tending the same purple-flowered shrub “beside the marble fountain” (Hawthorne).</Card>
<Card n="3" head="The effect">Giovanni crosses from one chronotope into the other every time he leans out his window — and the story tracks his growing inability to tell which time, and which self, is real.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
One concept from Ribó's opening pages of the setting chapter deserves its own moment: the chronotope, a term he borrows from the Russian theorist Mikhail Bakhtin. The idea is that space and time are never really separate in narrative — they're fused into unified chronotopes, distinct zones with their own rules for how time moves. Watch what Hawthorne does with this. The university, where Giovanni studies and where Professor Baglioni lectures, runs on linear, accumulating, rational time — the forward march of scientific inquiry. But the garden beneath Giovanni's window runs on an entirely different clock: cyclical, ritual time, marked by Rappaccini's daily rounds among his poisonous plants, always ending at the same purple-flowered shrub beside the ruined marble fountain. Giovanni's chamber sits at the seam between these two chronotopes, and every time he leans out that window, he crosses from one time-space into the other. The story's psychological disorientation — Giovanni's growing inability to trust his own senses — is inseparable from this structural fact: he is a man who cannot decide which chronotope, and which version of himself, is real.
-->

<!--
Ribó splits the representation of environment into two channels, and the first is topography: the objective layout of natural and artificial things in space, arranged as if — in his words — the setting 'could stand on its own,' like a stage, once all the characters have left it. Let's actually map Hawthorne's stage. At its center sits a ruined marble fountain, which the narrator describes as 'sculptured with rare art, but so wofully shattered that it was impossible to trace the original design from the chaos of remaining fragments.' Beside that broken fountain grows the story's central object: a shrub 'hung with purple gems' that Rappaccini alone approaches wearing a protective mask over his mouth and nostrils. That's the topography in miniature — a classical ruin, cracked and overgrown, presided over by an artificial, cultivated menace. Even stripped of characters, this layout tells a story: a lost order, classical beauty in fragments, and something new and dangerous planted at its exact center.
-->

eyebrow: "Ribó 3.2, continued"
title: "Atmosphere: Beauty Laced with Dread"
---

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Ribó's definition">Atmosphere is the arrangement of things “not as they stand on their own, but in their association with characters’ actions, thoughts, feelings, and experiences” (37) — the difference geographers draw between mere “space” and lived “place.”</Card>
<Card n="2" head="Hawthorne's technique">Sustained oxymoron: gorgeousness that repels, sunlight that feels unnatural, flowers whose “vivid colors” Giovanni cannot decide whether to call “beautiful” or “diseased.”</Card>
<Card n="3" head="Whose feeling is it?">The atmosphere is not the garden's; it is Giovanni's — filtered entirely through a spectator who does not yet know whether he is falling in love or being poisoned.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
If topography is the garden as object, atmosphere is the garden as experience — what Ribó describes as the arrangement of things not as they stand on their own, but in their association with a character's actions, thoughts, and feelings. He borrows a useful distinction here from the geographer Yi-Fu Tuan: the difference between abstract 'space' and lived, meaningful 'place.' Hawthorne saturates his descriptions of the garden with a very specific atmospheric technique: sustained oxymoron. The flowers are gorgeous and repellent in the same breath. The sunlight feels wrong. Giovanni himself cannot decide whether the vivid colors around him signal beauty or disease — and that uncertainty is the atmosphere. It doesn't belong to the garden; it belongs to Giovanni, the frightened, fascinated spectator through whom we experience every leaf of this place. Ribó would call this a textbook case of atmospheric setting: description doing the work of psychology, without a single line of direct commentary from the narrator.
-->

<Cards>
<Card n="1">Hawthorne takes this claim literally: Beatrice's body is chemically continuous with the garden's poison.</Card>
<Card n="2">She calls the purple shrub “my sister, my splendor” (Hawthorne) — kinship language, not ownership language.</Card>
<Card n="3">This is not metaphor layered onto a separate character. In Ribó's terms, character and environment have collapsed into a single existent.</Card>

<!--
Here is the claim from Ribó's chapter that I think unlocks the entire story. He writes: 'environments are more than just an objective background… at a fundamental level, environments are the meaningful entanglements of characters with their own world.' In most fiction, that's a useful metaphor — a way of saying that setting reflects or colors a character. Hawthorne, remarkably, makes it literally true. Beatrice's body is not merely surrounded by poison; it is constituted by poison, sustained by the same substance that kills every other living thing that touches the garden. She addresses the purple shrub not as property but as kin — she calls it, quote, 'my sister, my splendor.' That is not ornamental language. In Ribó's vocabulary, character and environment have stopped being two separate existents linked by association and have become a single existent: Beatrice is not in the garden. Beatrice is, in some sense, of the garden. That fusion is what makes her death, when it comes, a case study in exactly the entanglement Ribó describes.
-->

<!--
With topography and atmosphere established, Ribó proposes a four-part typology of how setting relates to the rest of the story: irrelevant, functional, mental, and symbolic. He's careful to call this an orientation rather than a rigid taxonomy — many stories blend categories, and that is exactly what we're about to find in Hawthorne. Irrelevant setting, where the narrator gives minimal information and characters seem to move through neutral, featureless space, is easy to dismiss here: nothing in this story is spatially neutral. But the other three kinds — functional, mental, and symbolic — are all genuinely, simultaneously present in Rappaccini's garden. That layering is unusual, and it's worth pausing on: most stories lean on one dominant kind of setting. Hawthorne runs all three at once, in the same eight or nine pages. Let's take each in turn, with a specific textual moment attached to it.
-->

---

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Ribó's definition">Functional setting provides “only the information needed to sustain the story” — topography in service of plot mechanics (39–40).</Card>
<Card n="2" head="The textual mechanism">A lizard dies from a drop of moisture shaken from the shrub; an insect drops dead in the air near Beatrice's breath — small, precise demonstrations of how the poison actually operates.</Card>
<Card n="3" head="Why it matters structurally">These are functional details that double as Chekhov's gun: once we've seen the poison work on a lizard, its working on a person is no longer implausible — it's inevitable.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
Our first applied case: functional setting, which Ribó defines as setting that provides only the information a story actually needs to keep its plot and characters moving. Hawthorne gives us two small, almost clinical demonstrations of how the garden's poison works: a lizard dies from a single drop of moisture shaken loose from the purple shrub, and an insect drops dead in midair near Beatrice's breath. Neither moment is atmospheric or symbolic on its own — it is functional in the plainest sense, establishing the mechanics of a poison that will later matter enormously to the plot. But notice what this functional information quietly does: once we've watched the poison kill a lizard, its killing a man doesn't require justification later. This is functional setting operating exactly like what Ribó, in his chapter on verisimilitude, will call Chekhov's gun — a detail introduced because the story will need it to go off.
-->

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Ribó's definition">Mental setting is presented “from the perspective of one of the characters, as a kind of inner experience or landscape of the mind” (40).</Card>
<Card n="2" head="The textual mechanism">Giovanni, watching from his window, asks himself directly: “Am I awake? Have I my senses?… What is this being?” (Hawthorne) — the garden becomes indistinguishable from a fever-dream.</Card>
<Card n="3" head="Why it matters structurally">The reader is denied a stable, external description of the garden. We only ever get Giovanni's garden — which means every fact we “know” about Beatrice arrives already contaminated by his doubt.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
Our second applied case: mental setting — setting presented, in Ribó's words, 'from the perspective of one of the characters, as a kind of inner experience or landscape of the mind.' Hawthorne gives us the clearest possible signal that we've entered this mode when Giovanni, watching Beatrice from his window, stops and asks himself outright: quote, 'Am I awake? Have I my senses?… What is this being?' That is not narratorial description. That is a character interrogating his own perception in real time, and it means we, as readers, never actually get an objective, external view of this garden at all. We only ever get Giovanni's garden — filtered through his escalating fear, desire, and suspicion. Ribó's point about mental setting is that it's often used, quote, 'to highlight the inner life of the main character and heighten the identification of the reader.' Hawthorne uses it for something slightly crueler: to make us share Giovanni's epistemic panic, so that when he eventually makes the worst possible interpretive mistake, we understand exactly how he got there.
-->

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Ribó's definition">Symbolic setting establishes “a meaningful relationship” between environment and theme — setting that exists to “represent abstract ideas” (40).</Card>
<Card n="2" head="The textual mechanism">Giovanni names the allegory himself: “Was this garden, then, the Eden of the present world?—and this man… was he the Adam?” (Hawthorne).</Card>
<Card n="3" head="Why it matters structurally">A garden, a forbidden central shrub, a maker who is also a kind of god, a fall accomplished through knowledge — the symbolic register doesn't hide beneath the surface here. Hawthorne stages it explicitly, then lets Rappaccini's science replace Genesis's God.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
Our third and final applied case: symbolic setting, which Ribó defines as setting arranged to give prominence to other elements of the story — plot, characterization, or theme — by building a meaningful relationship between them and a particular environment. Hawthorne, characteristically, doesn't bury this one. He has Giovanni say it outright: quote, 'Was this garden, then, the Eden of the present world? — and this man… was he the Adam?' The allegory is on the surface: a garden, a forbidden central plant instead of a forbidden tree, a maker who occupies the position of a god, and a fall accomplished not through disobedience but through cultivated, deliberate knowledge. What makes this more than a simple Genesis retelling is what replaces the serpent: not temptation, but science practiced without what Professor Baglioni calls regard for 'mankind.' Rappaccini's garden restages Eden with the biological sciences standing where faith used to stand — and that substitution is the story's real theme, delivered entirely through setting.
-->

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="The problem literary narrative solves">“Unlike movies, literary narratives cannot represent setting directly… It rests on readers to recreate in their own imagination those environments” (Ribó 40).</Card>
<Card n="2" head="Where Hawthorne sits on the spectrum">Ribó contrasts Hemingway's economy with Proust's density, description “long, meticulous and intricate like a tapestry” (41). Hawthorne's garden catalog — page after page of named, gorgeous, unnatural flowers — leans toward the tapestry.</Card>
<Card n="3" head="The payoff">Density here is not indulgence. Every reader must personally construct an image of flowers that are simultaneously beautiful and lethal — which means every reader performs, privately, the same category confusion Giovanni suffers.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
Chapter three's fourth section turns from what setting is to how prose actually builds it: description. Ribó makes an obvious but important point — unlike film, literary narrative cannot show us a setting directly. It rests on readers, in his words, to 'recreate in their own imagination those environments' based purely on the words provided. He places writers on a spectrum, from Hemingway's spare, quick description to Proust's description, which he calls, quote, 'long, meticulous and intricate like a tapestry.' Hawthorne's garden catalog leans hard toward the Proustian end: page after page of named, lush, faintly wrong flowers, described with a density that slows the story down on purpose. That density is not self-indulgence. Because every reader must personally construct a mental image of blossoms that are simultaneously gorgeous and venomous, every reader is quietly forced to perform the same perceptual confusion that torments Giovanni. Hawthorne's description doesn't just depict the problem of beautiful poison. It makes us solve that problem, privately, in our own imagination, every time we read.
-->

eyebrow: "Ribó 3.5 — “Verisimilitude,” part 1"

---

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Chekhov's gun">“If you say in the first chapter that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third chapter it absolutely must go off” (qtd. in Ribó 43).</Card>
<Card n="2" head="Barthes' counter-case">Ribó also cites Roland Barthes on “useless details” — insignificant description that exists only to make a fiction feel real (43–44).</Card>
<Card n="3" head="Hawthorne uses both, on purpose">The purple shrub is a loaded gun that fires exactly on schedule. The fountain's ruin, by contrast, need not matter to the plot at all — it simply makes Padua feel like a real, aged, decaying place.</Card>
</Cards>

<!--
Chapter three's final section, on verisimilitude, opens with the difference between two kinds of narrative detail. The first is what Ribó, quoting Chekhov, calls the loaded gun: quote, 'if you say in the first chapter that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third chapter it absolutely must go off.' The purple shrub is exactly this kind of detail — introduced early, tended obsessively, and, by the story's end, entirely responsible for its catastrophe. But Ribó also cites the theorist Roland Barthes on the opposite phenomenon: 'useless details,' description that serves no plot function at all and exists only to make a fictional world feel convincingly, boringly real. Hawthorne gives us one of these too — the ruined marble fountain doesn't need to be broken for the plot to work. Its shattered, untraceable design does something quieter: it tells us this garden has a history, that time has passed here before Giovanni ever arrived. One detail is loaded. One detail is just weather on old stone. Hawthorne, remarkably, uses both kinds in the very same paragraph.
-->

<Cards cols="1">
<Card n="1">Verisimilitude is “the attempt by narrative discourse to convince readers that the storyworld is a faithful imitation… of the ‘real’ world” (Ribó 44).</Card>
<Card n="2">Hawthorne stacks two verisimilitude strategies: dense, tapestry-like description of Padua and the garden, and the frame device attributing the whole tale to “M. de l'Aubépine.”</Card>

<!--
Which brings us to verisimilitude itself — Ribó's term, from the Latin for 'truth-likeness,' for narrative discourse's attempt to convince us that a storyworld is a faithful imitation of the real one, regardless of whether it's literally true. Hawthorne deploys two verisimilitude strategies at once. The first is the dense, tapestry-like description we just discussed — realistic enough about Padua and its university culture to anchor an impossible premise in a recognizable place. The second is that translator's frame we noted at the very start: Hawthorne distances himself from his own tale by attributing it to the invented Frenchman Monsieur de l'Aubépine, a fictional editorial mask that makes the story feel like a document rather than an invention. And here is the elegant closing turn: that authorial mask mirrors, almost exactly, the plot itself. Rappaccini literally wears a mask to handle what he's made dangerous. Baglioni's science wears the mask of concern for humanity while serving something closer to professional rivalry. Verisimilitude, in this story, is not a neutral technique. It's the same trick the characters play on each other, performed one level up, by the narrative discourse itself.
-->

title: "The Garden as Meaning-Machine"
---

<Cards>
<Card n="1" head="Setting is not where the plot happens">It is the mechanism that manufactures the plot: the poison's topography produces every subsequent event, from the dying lizard to the fatal antidote.</Card>
<Card n="2" head="Setting is not separate from character">Beatrice and the garden are, per Ribó's own claim, “entangled” rather than merely adjacent — she is the environment's clearest human expression.</Card>
<Card n="3" head="Setting carries the theme">The Eden allegory, the mask motif, and the topography/atmosphere split all converge on one argument: knowledge pursued without regard for the person it acts upon becomes indistinguishable from poison.</Card>

<!--
Let's draw these threads together. We began with a claim from Ribó's opening pages of chapter three: that environments are not objective backgrounds but meaningful entanglements between characters and their world. Everything we've examined today confirms that claim in an unusually literal way. The garden is not where this plot happens — it is the mechanism that generates the plot, from the lizard's death to the fatal logic of the antidote. The garden is not separate from Beatrice — in Ribó's vocabulary, character and environment have become entangled almost past the point of separation. And the garden is not decoration for a theme stated elsewhere — it carries the theme directly, through the Eden allegory, through the recurring mask motif, through the very split between topography and atmosphere that Ribó gives us as an analytic tool. The argument the setting makes, finally, is this: knowledge pursued without regard for the person it acts upon becomes, functionally, indistinguishable from poison. Hawthorne never states that thesis as commentary. He grows it, literally, in a garden.
